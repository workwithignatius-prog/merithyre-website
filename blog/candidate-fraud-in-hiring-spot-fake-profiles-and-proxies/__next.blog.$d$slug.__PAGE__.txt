28:T4df6,<p>Fake profiles and stand-in interviewees can reach your shortlist. See the red flags to watch for and how transcripts and scoring build real evidence.</p>
<p>A candidate joins your video call. The resume looks sharp, and the answers sound polished. Weeks after the offer, the work does not match what you saw.</p>
<p>This is no longer a rare story. Recruiters now face invented profiles, stand-in interviewees, and AI-written answers. Many of these reach the interview stage before anyone raises a doubt.</p>
<p>This guide explains what candidate fraud looks like and where it slips through sourcing and screening. It lists red flags for fake profiles and proxy interviews. It also shows how transcripts and structured scoring turn a gut feeling into evidence.</p>
<p><strong>Quick answer: How do you spot fake profiles and proxy interviews?</strong></p>
<p>Verify identity before the first live interview. Compare answers across every stage for consistency. Score each interview against the same criteria and keep transcripts as a record. No single check catches everything, so layer several checks together.</p>
<h2 id="what-candidate-fraud-looks-like-in-modern-hiring">What Candidate Fraud Looks Like in Modern Hiring</h2>
<p>Candidate fraud covers several different problems. Each one needs a different check. Sorting them first helps your team match the right control to the right risk.</p>
<h3>Fake Profiles and Fabricated Credentials</h3>
<p>A fake profile describes a person who does not exist as presented. Some are fully invented. Others borrow the name and personal data of a real individual.</p>
<p>Generative AI has made this cheap. A fraudster can build a resume, a profile, and a cover letter in minutes. Gartner says generative AI makes fake identities and forged documents easier to produce. It also makes sending a proxy to a video interview simpler. <a href="https://www.greenhouse.com/guidance/mitigate-rising-candidate-fraud-through-identity-verification">greenhouse</a></p>
<p>Fake credentials follow the same pattern. Degrees, certificates, and job titles can be invented or inflated. A resume alone cannot prove any of them.</p>
<h3>Proxy Interviews and Stand-Ins</h3>
<p>A proxy interview happens when someone else takes the interview for the candidate. The stand-in may be a skilled friend or a paid expert. Their goal is to pass the round on the candidate’s behalf.</p>
<p>Two outcomes follow. The real candidate may start work without the skills shown in the interview. In some cases, a different person takes the job entirely.</p>
<p>Proxy fraud also appears in online tests and coding assessments. The person who sat the test may never appear again.</p>
<h2 id="deepfakes-and-live-ai-assistance">Deepfakes and Live AI Assistance</h2>
<p>Deepfakes use AI to place one person’s face or voice onto another. The FBI warned about this in June 2022. Applicants used deepfakes and stolen personal data to seek remote jobs. The roles named were in IT, programming, databases, and software.</p>
<p>The FBI noted that lip movements on screen did not always match the audio. Coughs and sneezes also failed to line up with the video. These gaps can help interviewers today. The technology keeps improving, so do not rely on it alone.</p>
<p>Live AI assistance is a quieter form of the same problem. A candidate may read answers from a tool during the call. The candidate is present, yet the knowledge on display belongs to the tool.</p>
<h3>How Common Is Candidate Fraud?</h3>
<p>Survey data gives a rough picture. Figures vary with wording and sample size. Treat them as signals, not exact counts.</p>
<ul><li>A <a href="https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-07-31-gartner-survey-shows-just-26-percent-of-job-applicants-trust-ai-will-fairly-evaluate-them">2025 Gartner survey</a> of 3,000 candidates found 6% admitted to interview fraud. That includes posing as someone else or having someone pose for them.</li><li><a href="https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-07-31-gartner-survey-shows-just-26-percent-of-job-applicants-trust-ai-will-fairly-evaluate-them">Gartner</a> predicts one in four candidate profiles worldwide will be fake by 2028.</li><li><a href="https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2026">Checkr surveyed</a> 3,000 US managers involved in hiring in 2026. Of them, 40% had interviewed a candidate later found using a fake identity. The 2025 figure was 31%.</li><li>In the same survey, <a href="https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2026">67% of managers</a> had confirmed or strongly suspected an interview proxy. That figure was 35% in 2025.</li><li>In <a href="https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2025">Checkr’s 2025 survey</a>, 23% of managers reported losses above $50,000 from hiring or identity fraud in one year.</li></ul>
<p>The Gartner 2028 number is a forecast, not a count. The Checkr figures come from manager reports, which can include suspicion. Even so, every source points in the same direction.</p>
<h2 id="why-fraud-slips-through-sourcing-and-screening">Why Fraud Slips Through Sourcing and Screening</h2>
<p>Fraud succeeds where the process is thin. Most teams build screening to rank skills, not to confirm identity. Three gaps explain most of the misses.</p>
<h3>Identity Checks Come Too Late</h3>
<p><a href="https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2026#:~:text=Part%20of%20the,the%20closest%20attention.">Checkr’s 2026 survey</a> found only 29% of companies verify identity at application. Another 49% wait until after at least one live interview. By then, a fake candidate has already used recruiter and manager time.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.greenhouse.com/guidance/mitigate-rising-candidate-fraud-through-identity-verification">Gartner’s 2024 survey</a> found only 7% of recruiting leaders used identity document validation software. Standard background checks also arrive late. They rely on details the candidate supplied in the first place.</p>
<h3>Resume Screens Reward Polish</h3>
<p>Screening tools score what a document says. They cannot confirm that the writer is real. A resume tuned to a job post can rank high with no real experience behind it.</p>
<p>AI screening reads context better than keyword filters do. That helps surface qualified people. It does not replace a check on who the person is.</p>
<h3>Speed Pressure and Thin Evidence</h3>
<p>Open roles create pressure. Checkr found more than one in four managers moved a candidate forward despite suspecting dishonesty, because the role had to be filled fast.</p>
<p>Weak records add to the problem. Interview notes often say “good communicator” and little else. Without specifics, a doubt cannot become a documented concern. Only 23% of managers say they are extremely confident their process would catch a fraudulent candidate.</p>
<h2 id="red-flags-that-signal-a-fake-profile">Red Flags That Signal a Fake Profile</h2>
<p>Red flags show recruiters where to look closer. None of them prove fraud alone. Treat a cluster of flags as a reason to verify.</p>
<h3>Profile and Resume Red Flags</h3>
<ul><li>A profile created recently, with few connections and little activity.</li><li>A career path that jumps in seniority without a clear reason.</li><li>Employers that are hard to find, or dates that conflict with public records.</li><li>Resume wording that mirrors your job post almost line by line.</li><li>A profile photo that looks edited, generic, or reused across accounts.</li><li>Long skills lists with no matching project detail.</li></ul>
<h3>Application and Contact Red Flags</h3>
<ul><li>Reluctance to take a phone call or turn on a camera.</li><li>Contact details that change between applications under the same name.</li><li>A stated location that conflicts with the candidate’s time zone and availability.</li><li>Last-minute requests to move the interview to another device or link.</li><li>References who reply only by email, or whose details link back to the candidate.</li></ul>
<p>Keep every flag tied to behavior and records. Do not judge names, accents, or regions. Those signals add bias and prove nothing.</p>
<h2 id="warning-signs-of-a-proxy-interview">Warning Signs of a Proxy Interview</h2>
<p>Proxy interviews leave traces. Interviewers who know what to watch for can catch many of them. The signs fall into three groups.</p>
<h3>Behavior on Camera and Audio</h3>
<ul><li>Lip movement that lags behind the voice.</li><li>Eyes that move to a second screen before each answer.</li><li>Long pauses followed by smooth, scripted delivery.</li><li>Typing sounds or a reading rhythm in the voice.</li><li>Coughs or sneezes that do not match the video.</li><li>A camera angle that hides the candidate’s hands or desk.</li></ul>
<h3>Answer Patterns</h3>
<p>Strong stand-ins know the subject well. They struggle with the personal details of the resume. Probe those details.</p>
<ul><li>Clear theory, but thin detail about their own projects.</li><li>Trouble explaining why they chose one approach over another.</li><li>Answers that fall apart when you change the scenario slightly.</li><li>Phrasing that matches common online interview answers.</li></ul>
<p>Try asking, “What went wrong on that project, and what did you change?” Real experience produces specific, untidy answers. Scripts produce clean ones.</p>
<h3>Mismatches Across Stages</h3>
<p>The strongest signal often appears across rounds. Compare each stage against the earlier ones.</p>
<ul><li>Voice or communication style differs between the screening call and the final round.</li><li>Test results sit far above or below interview performance.</li><li>The person on day one looks different from the person you interviewed.</li><li>Login locations or devices conflict with what the candidate stated.</li></ul>

<h2 id="build-a-layered-verification-process">Build a Layered Verification Process</h2>
<p>No single check stops fraud. <a href="https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-07-31-gartner-survey-shows-just-26-percent-of-job-applicants-trust-ai-will-fairly-evaluate-them">Gartner</a> recommends a multi-layer strategy that includes using assessments to uncover fraud. Each layer raises the effort a fraudster must spend.</p>
<h3>Verify Identity Before the First Live Call</h3>
<p>Move identity checks earlier in the funnel. Confirm that the person matches the documents they present. Do this before you spend interview time.</p>
<ul><li>Use the same identity check for every candidate in the role.</li><li>Follow local privacy and consent rules for ID and biometric data.</li><li>Store only the data you need, and set a clear retention limit.</li></ul>
<p>A uniform check also lowers bias risk. No candidate is singled out by a hunch.</p>
<h3>Ask Questions That Need Real Experience</h3>
<p>Build questions around the candidate’s own work. Ask about decisions, trade-offs, and mistakes. Then ask a second and a third follow-up.</p>
<p>Follow-ups are where stand-ins and scripts struggle. They prepared the first answer. They rarely prepared the third.</p>
<h3>Repeat Checks at Later Stages</h3>
<p>Fraud can appear after the offer. <a href="https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/majority-of-job-seekers-wary-of-ai-gartner-says">Checkr’s 2026 report</a> argues that fraud checks must continue past the offer stage. Gartner also advises extending fraud prevention beyond the initial hiring phase.</p>
<p>Re-confirm identity at the final round and again at onboarding. Compare the person on screen with earlier rounds. Limit system access until all checks clear.</p>

<h2 id="why-transcripts-and-structured-scoring-work-as-evidence">Why Transcripts and Structured Scoring Work as Evidence</h2>
<p>A suspicion cannot support a decision on its own. Recruiters need records that show what happened and when. Transcripts and structured scoring provide that record.</p>
<h3>Transcripts Create a Reviewable Record</h3>
<p>Memory fades after a day of interviews. Notes capture impressions, not exact words. A transcript captures what the candidate actually said.</p>
<p>With transcripts, reviewers can compare rounds side by side. They can spot a project described two different ways. They can also see answers that repeat the same stock phrases.</p>
<p>MeritHyre attaches transcripts to each candidate record. Reviewers can return to the exact passage behind a score. That record supports a fair second look when a recruiter and manager disagree.</p>
<h3>Structured Scoring Makes Comparison Fair</h3>
<p>Structured scoring rates every candidate on the same criteria. Each rating links to evidence in the transcript. Reviewers can see why a score was given.</p>
<p>This helps in two ways. A strong resume paired with a weak interview score becomes visible. Every candidate also faces the same rubric, which limits personal bias.</p>

<h3>Human Review Keeps Decisions Fair</h3>
<p>Transcripts and scores show evidence. They do not verify identity alone. Pair them with identity checks and reference calls.</p>
<p>Recruiters should also guard against false alarms. Non-native speakers may pause or repeat themselves. Nerves can look like evasion. Judge the content of answers, not accent or style. Use a flag to start a conversation, not to reject someone.</p>
<h2 id="balancing-fraud-prevention-with-candidate-trust">Balancing Fraud Prevention with Candidate Trust</h2>
<p>Strict checks can push away honest applicants. Candidates already feel cautious about AI in hiring. A <a href="https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-07-31-gartner-survey-shows-just-26-percent-of-job-applicants-trust-ai-will-fairly-evaluate-them">Gartner survey</a> found only 26% of candidates trust AI to evaluate them fairly. Clear communication protects that trust.</p>
<ul><li>Tell candidates which checks you run and why.</li><li>State what AI use is acceptable during the process.</li><li>Explain how long you keep identity and interview data.</li><li>Offer a simple route to resolve a false flag.</li></ul>
<p>Gartner also found 62% of candidates were more likely to apply when a role required in-person interviews. This suggests many candidates accept reasonable verification steps. Fair, open checks cost little with honest applicants.</p>
<h3>Final Thoughts</h3>
<p>Candidate fraud is a screening problem as much as an interview problem. Early identity checks, probing questions, and layered reviews reduce the risk. Transcripts and structured scores give your team proof to act on.</p>
<p>Start with one high-risk role, such as a remote technical position. Add checks at each stage and track the results. Then extend the process to other roles.</p>
<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What is candidate fraud in hiring?</h3>
<p>Candidate fraud is any attempt to deceive an employer during hiring. It includes fake profiles, stolen identities, falsified credentials, and proxy interviews. Some candidates also hide AI assistance during assessments. The common thread is a mismatch between the person or skills presented and the person who actually applies.</p>
<h3>What is a proxy interview?</h3>
<p>A proxy interview happens when someone else takes the interview for the candidate. The stand-in answers every question on their behalf. The real candidate may then start work without the skills shown. In some cases, a different person takes the job.</p>
<h3>How can you spot a fake candidate profile?</h3>
<p>Fake profiles leave patterns across records and behavior. Check for these signs before you schedule an interview.</p>
<ul><li>A new profile with few connections or little activity.</li><li>Employers or dates that conflict with public records.</li><li>Resume wording that copies your job post.</li><li>Contact details that change between applications.</li><li>Refusal to take a call or turn on a camera.</li></ul>
<h3>What are the signs of a proxy interview?</h3>
<p>Proxy interviews often show gaps between what a candidate claims and how they respond. Watch for these signals during and after the call.</p>
<ul><li>Voice and lip movement that do not match.</li><li>Eyes that shift to another screen before answers.</li><li>Thin detail about projects on the resume.</li><li>Performance that changes sharply between rounds.</li><li>Test results far above interview ability.</li></ul>
<h3>How can recruiters prevent candidate fraud?</h3>
<p>Prevention works best when checks appear at every stage. These steps give a practical starting point.</p>
<ul><li>Verify identity before the first live interview.</li><li>Ask follow-up questions on real project details.</li><li>Score every interview against the same criteria.</li><li>Keep transcripts of each interview round.</li><li>Re-check identity at the final round and onboarding.</li></ul>
<h3>Why do transcripts help detect candidate fraud?</h3>
<p>Transcripts record exactly what a candidate said in each round. Reviewers can compare answers across stages. They can spot stock phrases and changing project details. Notes written from memory cannot offer that level of detail. Scores link to specific passages, so teams have evidence before acting on a suspicion.</p>
<h3>How does MeritHyre help with candidate fraud?</h3>
<p>MeritHyre pairs interview transcripts with structured scoring for every candidate. Each score ties back to what the candidate said. Recruiters can compare rounds, find gaps, and document concerns. This gives teams a clear record to act on. Final decisions stay with the recruiter, who also runs separate identity checks.</p>
<h3>Does candidate fraud only affect remote roles?</h3>
<p>No. Remote hiring carries more exposure because interviews and onboarding happen without in-person contact. In-person and hybrid roles can still see fake credentials, inflated resumes, and coached answers.
