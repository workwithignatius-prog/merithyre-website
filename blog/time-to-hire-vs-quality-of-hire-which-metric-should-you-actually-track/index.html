Time-to-Hire vs Quality-of-Hire: Which Metric Should You Actually Track?

A side-by-side look at the two hiring metrics, the warning signs that speed is winning over quality, and a five-step way to start measuring how good your hires really are.

Fast hires are easy. Good hires are the ones you can measure. Here is what each metric tells you, where each one misleads, and how to track both without building a data team.

Introduction

Track both, but treat them differently. Quality-of-hire is the goal. Time-to-hire is the guardrail. Speed tells you whether your process is efficient; quality tells you whether the process was worth running.

Most hiring dashboards lead with time-to-hire because it is easy to count. The dates already exist in your applicant tracking system, the number updates every week, and leadership understands “days”. Quality-of-hire is the opposite: it takes months to produce a single data point and needs input from people outside recruiting.

That difference creates a quiet bias. The metric that is easy to see gets managed, and the metric that matters gets ignored. A team can cut its time-to-hire in half and look like it improved, while the people it hired in a hurry leave within a year. The dashboard never shows the cost.

The rule of thumb: use time-to-hire to find where your process waits. Use quality-of-hire to decide whether what you are doing is working. If the two ever conflict, quality wins.

What Time-to-Hire Really Measures

Time-to-hire sounds simple, but two different numbers hide under the same name, and mixing them up is a common reporting mistake.

  • Time-to-fill: days from the moment a job opening is approved or posted to the moment an offer is accepted. It measures how long a seat stays empty.
  • Time-to-hire: days from the moment a specific candidate enters your pipeline (applies or is first contacted) to the moment that candidate accepts. It measures how fast your process moves one person.

Time-to-fill matters to the business, because an empty seat has a cost. Time-to-hire matters to the candidate, because a slow process is when strong applicants take other offers.

What Speed Is Good For

Time-to-hire is excellent at exposing waiting. If a candidate sits for six days between the screen and the first interview, the delay is almost never the work itself. It is scheduling, slow feedback or an unclear owner. Those are fixable, and fixing them makes the experience better without lowering the standard.

Where Speed Misleads

Speed cannot tell a fast good hire from a fast bad one. A number that drops can mean you removed waiting, or it can mean you skipped steps: shorter interviews, fewer questions, no reference check. Time-to-hire gives no way to tell those apart.

What Quality-of-Hire Really Measures

Quality-of-hire asks a single question: how well did the person we hired actually do? There is no universal formula, but most teams build it from a few indicators collected after the hire.

  • Performance: a manager rating after the first 6 to 12 months, on the same scale for every hire in a role.
  • Retention: whether the hire is still in the role at 6 and 12 months.
  • Manager satisfaction: a short 1 to 5 score collected at around 90 days: “Would you hire this person again?”
  • Ramp time: how long the hire took to reach full productivity, compared with what the team expected.

Score each indicator on the same scale, then average them. The result is a single number per hire that you can compare across roles, teams and months.

Quality-of-hire score = (performance + retention + manager satisfaction + ramp) ÷ number of indicators used

Use the same 1-to-5 scale for every indicator, so no single one dominates.

The Catch: It Is Slow, and It Needs the Interview Data

Quality-of-hire only becomes useful when you can connect it back to what you knew at the time of hire. If you compare a hire’s first-year performance to the interview scores that predicted it, you learn which competencies actually forecast success in your company and which are noise. Without recorded interview evidence, there is nothing to compare against, and quality-of-hire turns into a vague feeling.

That is why the two guides fit together. A structured interview scorecard gives you the “before” data, and quality-of-hire gives you the “after”. Research supports the link: a 2022 meta-analysis by Sackett and colleagues found structured interviews to be among the stronger predictors of job performance, with a validity of roughly 0.42 compared to 0.19 for unstructured interviews.

5 Signs You're Optimizing for Speed Instead of Quality

  • Your dashboard has days, but no outcomes. You can report time-to-hire by role, but not how last year’s hires are performing.
  • Shortlists are decided on gut feel. Debriefs sound like “good vibe” and “not sure about fit” instead of scored evidence.
  • Interviews differ for every candidate. Different questions, different scales, no way to compare.
  • Early attrition is treated as bad luck. Nobody traces departures back to how the person was hired.
  • Hiring managers rarely give feedback after the hire. If nobody asks “was this a good hire?”, the data does not exist.

Seeing two or three of these does not mean your team is doing something wrong. It usually means the process was built for speed, because speed was the only thing anyone could measure. The fix is to add measurement, not to slow down.

How to Start Tracking Quality-of-Hire in Five Steps

You do not need new software to begin. A spreadsheet with one row per hire is enough to get the first six months of data.

  1. Pick three indicators, not ten. Start with manager satisfaction at 90 days, retention at 6 and 12 months, and a first-year performance rating. Add ramp time later.
  2. Use one scale for everyone. Rate every indicator from 1 to 5 with a short written meaning for each level. Without that, a “4” from one manager means something different from a “4” from another.
  3. Record the interview evidence at the time of hire. Save the scorecard, the competency scores and the notes for every hire. This is the “before” data you will compare against later.
  4. Set a check-in calendar. Book the 90-day manager survey and the 6 and 12 month reviews when the person starts, so collecting the data does not depend on someone remembering.
  5. Review quarterly and compare. Every quarter, compare quality scores against time-to-hire and interview scores. Look for patterns: which sources produce the best hires, which competencies predict success, and where faster hiring started to cost quality.

Where AI Helps

The hardest part of quality-of-hire is not the math. It is capturing consistent evidence at the start, and then keeping it connected to outcomes months later. People forget, templates drift and notes go missing.

AI-assisted hiring helps here because it applies the same questions, the same scale and the same record-keeping to every candidate. That gives you comparable interview evidence for every hire, which is exactly what a quality-of-hire review needs. It also reduces the admin work that makes speed so tempting in the first place. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between time-to-hire and time-to-fill?

Time-to-fill counts the days from when a job opening is approved or posted to when an offer is accepted. Time-to-hire counts the days from when a specific candidate enters your pipeline to when that candidate accepts. One measures how long a seat is empty; the other measures how fast your process moves a candidate.

How do you measure quality of hire?

Combine three or four indicators for each hire: a performance rating after 6 to 12 months, retention at 6 and 12 months, a manager satisfaction score at around 90 days, and ramp time. Score each on the same 1-to-5 scale and average them.

Which metric should I track first?

Track both, with quality as the goal and speed as the guardrail. Time-to-hire is easy to start today. Quality-of-hire takes months to produce data, so begin collecting it now.

Can small teams measure quality of hire without special software?

Yes. A spreadsheet with one row per hire is enough: the interview scores at hire, a 90-day manager rating, a first-year performance rating and a retention flag. The hard part is recording interview evidence consistently, which structured scorecards help with.

Does a faster hiring process reduce quality?

Not automatically. Speed becomes a problem when it comes from skipping evidence, such as cutting interview structure or screening on gut feel. Removing waiting time, like scheduling delays and slow feedback, can speed up hiring without lowering quality.
